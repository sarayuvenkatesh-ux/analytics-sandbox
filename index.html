Six percent of policies file a claim. Finding which six is the whole job.

Risk drivers

What actually moves claim risk

Each group holds roughly the same number of policies, so the claim rates are directly comparable. Amber bars sit above the 6.40% portfolio average.

Policy tenure

Quintiles · strongest single driver

Claim rate climbs from 3.6% to 8.7% — a 2.4× spread. Note this partly reflects exposure: a policy held longer has had more time to generate a claim.

Tenure × policyholder age

Quintiles · engineered interaction

The combination separates risk better than either parent alone — 3.5% to 8.8%, a 2.5× spread. This is the single most useful engineered feature.

Policyholder age

Quintiles · normalised scale

Risk rises with age, not the reverse. The oldest quintile claims at 7.1% against 5.6% for the youngest — a 1.3× spread.

Vehicle age

Quintiles · normalised scale

Older vehicles claim less. Plausibly a claiming-behaviour effect: minor damage on an old car is absorbed rather than claimed.

Geography

Sorted high to low. Read the policy counts alongside the rates — the extremes at both ends come from clusters holding only a few hundred policies, where a handful of claims moves the number several points.

Business questions

The three questions we set out to answer

Confirmed

Do policyholder age and policy tenure relate to claim likelihood?

Yes, and they are the two clearest drivers in the data. Tenure moves the claim rate from 3.6% to 8.7% across quintiles; policyholder age from 5.6% to 7.1%. Their interaction is stronger than either alone, and the model leans on tenure above everything else.

Rejected

Do vehicle safety attributes reduce claim frequency?

No measurable effect. NCAP rating is flat at 6.2%–6.7% across the whole scale, including unrated vehicles. Safety-equipment count, airbags and individual safety flags are all near-zero in the regression coefficients and contribute nothing in SHAP. The "young driver in an unsafe car" flag built to the classic underwriting definition actually claims less than the rest of the book — 5.7% against 6.6%.

Partly

Are certain geographies systematically riskier?

Partly. Cluster claim rates span 3.9% to 10.7%, but the extremes sit in clusters holding only 109–242 policies. Restricted to clusters above 3,000 policies the spread is a more modest 4.7%–7.7%. Geography earns a place in the model but not a pricing lever on its own.

Recommendations

What to do with this

The model produces a defensible ordering of risk. Every recommendation below is scoped to what an ordering can support.

  • Underwriting
    Work the Very High band first

    761 policies — 6.5% of the scored book — claim at 15.0%, 2.3× the portfolio average. That is a small enough queue for manual review to be economic, and concentrated enough for the review to pay for itself.

  • Pricing
    Treat tenure as a rating factor, not a loyalty discount

    Long-tenure policies claim at 8.6% against 5.1% for the rest — a 1.7× gap that current pricing does not reflect. Confirm the exposure component first: part of this effect is simply that older policies have had longer to produce a claim.

  • Product
    Stop pricing on crash-safety rating

    NCAP shows no relationship with claim frequency anywhere in this analysis. Any discount currently justified by safety rating is unsupported by the portfolio's own experience and should be re-derived or withdrawn.

  • Data
    Close the gap that is capping accuracy

    ROC-AUC of 0.646 is a ceiling set by the data, not the method — brute-force generation of 342 candidate interaction features improved on the hand-built set by nothing. Driving behaviour, prior claim history, mileage and telematics are the missing inputs.

  • Risk
    Monitor before you rely on it

    The versioned model ships with a drift report comparing live scoring data against the training distribution. Re-run it monthly and retrain when band claim rates stop increasing monotonically — that is the signal the ranking has stopped working.

Read the model with these limits in mind

  • Discrimination is modest. ROC-AUC 0.646 supports triage and prioritisation. It does not support automated decline, and it is not a rating engine.
  • Precision is low by design. At the default cut-off, roughly 11 policies are flagged for every genuine claim caught. Use the bands to rank a review queue, not to reject business.
  • Tenure carries exposure. Longer-held policies have had more time to claim. Isolating the true risk effect needs an exposure-adjusted target.
  • Small clusters are noisy. The highest and lowest geographic rates rest on a few hundred policies each and should not drive regional pricing.
  • Claim severity is out of scope. The model predicts whether a claim occurs, never how much it costs.

Method & reproducibility

Data
58,592 policies · 44 raw columns · 0 missing · 0 duplicates
Preparation
IQR winsorisation, log transform on density, 17 flags encoded, 9 columns one-hot encoded
Feature selection
14 near-duplicate pairs removed at |r| > 0.9, then iterative VIF elimination to VIF < 10
Split
80/20 stratified · 46,873 train / 11,719 test · claim rate preserved at 6.40%
Models
Logistic regression (balanced) · decision tree tuned with Optuna, 25 trials, 3-fold CV
Validation
5-fold stratified CV · Box-Tidwell linearity test · train/test AUC gap 0.035
Tracking
MLflow, two experiments · joblib artifacts with JSON metadata · Evidently drift report

SHAP TreeExplainer over all 11,719 scored policies
